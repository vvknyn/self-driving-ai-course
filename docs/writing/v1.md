# Writing standard for Zero2FSD (approved by Vivek, 2026-10-10)

Reader: an adult with rusty high-school maths and no machine learning. Every sentence must be understandable on a first read by that person.

## The six checks (apply to every paragraph)
1. **One new idea per paragraph.** If a paragraph introduces two terms, split it.
2. **Define before use.** A term may appear only after the sentence that explains it. Forward references ("you'll meet X later") are fine if X is named in plain words.
3. **Read every symbol aloud the first time.** `|e|` → "|e|, read *the size of e*: e with any minus sign dropped, so |+1.8| = |−1.8| = 1.8". Subscripts → "e₁ is the error at the first step".
4. **Concrete before abstract.** Number example first, then the general symbol.
5. **No hidden step.** If a reader would compute the number themselves and get something different (88.85 × 20 = 1777, text says 1778), add a collapsed derivation box. Format: `<details><summary>How we got $X$</summary>` + blank line + markdown/math + blank line + `</details>`. One-step obvious arithmetic stays inline ("3.6 ÷ 2 = 1.8 m").
6. **Explain every code name** at first appearance: what the object is (`tel` = the drive's telemetry, the log the simulator kept) and what the attribute is (`.lat` = the lateral-error column).
Also: short bolded question-style subheads ("Which side is plus?") over long run-on sections; tie new ideas back to the step loop (camera → decide → steer → move, every 0.05 s).

## Never
- Change a number, constant, or claim without re-verifying it in code (`zero2fsd/`) or by running the sim. Every derivation box's arithmetic is checked by running Python.
- Drop content to make it simpler — explain it instead.
- Add jargon to explain jargon.

## Worked example (approved calibration, lecture 0.1.1)

BEFORE:
> Left is positive everywhere in this course, for offsets, headings and steering angles. Hold on to that; every sign bug you will ever write comes from forgetting it.
>
> The lane is 3.6 m wide, so the yellow line is 1.8 m to your left and the white line 1.8 m to your right. When $|e|$ reaches 1.8 m the front axle is on a line, and the simulator ends the run.
>
> A drive is not one number but a **time series**: one value of $e$ at every step. Call them $e_1, e_2, \ldots, e_N$, where $N$ is the number of steps. With a step every 0.05 s you get 20 values per second, so one lap of `gentle`, which takes the provided car 88.85 s, is $N = 1778$ values. In the lab they arrive as a NumPy array called `tel.lat`.

AFTER:
**Which side is plus?**
Lateral error can be positive or negative, and the sign tells you the side. Plus means the car is left of the lane's centre; minus means right. Later you'll meet two more "which side" numbers: which way the car is pointing, and which way the front wheels are turned. They follow the same rule. Left is plus, right is minus.

**How far can the car drift?**
The lane is 3.6 m wide and the car aims for the middle, so each painted line is half the width away: 3.6 ÷ 2 = 1.8 m. The yellow line is at e = +1.8 m (left), and the white line is at e = −1.8 m (right). If the car goes past either line, the simulator stops the drive.

To say "past either line" in one go, the course writes |e|, read "the size of e". It is e with any minus sign dropped, so |+1.8| = 1.8 and |−1.8| = 1.8. "The drive stops when |e| goes past 1.8 m" just means "when the car is more than 1.8 m from the centre, on either side."

**A drive is a list of numbers**
Remember the loop at the top of this unit: every 0.05 s the camera takes a picture, the car steers, and it moves a little. One pass through that loop is a **step**. At every step the simulator writes down the car's lateral error, so a whole drive becomes a long list of errors, in order. A list of measurements taken one after another in time is called a **time series**.

To point at one entry in the list, you write its position as a small number below the e. So e₁ is the error at the first step, e₂ at the second, and so on. N stands for how many entries there are, so the last one is e_N.

One lap of `gentle` takes the provided car 88.85 s, which gives N = 1778 entries.

<details><summary>How we got $N = 1778$</summary>

The simulator takes a reading at the start of every step: at 0 s, 0.05 s, 0.10 s, … and finally at 88.85 s. From 0 to 88.85 s there are 88.85 ÷ 0.05 = 1777 gaps of 0.05 s. Think of fence posts: 1777 gaps need 1778 posts, one at time 0 plus one at the end of each gap. So N = 1777 + 1 = 1778.

</details>

In the lab you get this list as `tel.lat`. `tel` holds the drive's **telemetry**, the log the simulator kept, and `.lat` is the lateral-error column of that log. It's a NumPy array, which is Python's usual container for a long list of numbers you want to do maths on.

(Verified facts behind the example: DT = 0.05 `zero2fsd/sim/dynamics.py:8`; LANE_WIDTH = 3.6 `zero2fsd/sim/world.py:13`; OFF_ROAD_OFFSET = 1.8 checked as `abs(lat) > OFF_ROAD_OFFSET` `zero2fsd/car/runner.py:15`; rows recorded at t = k·DT from k = 0, loop breaks after recording, `runner.py` run().)
